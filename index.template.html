Inside the big fund families, an ESG label changes what a fund owns, not how it votes

Shelby L. Riggs · Stewardship analysis · SEC Form N-PX, 2023–2026

Every U.S. mutual fund and ETF must tell the SEC how it voted at every company meeting. This report reads three years of those filings for {{head.funds}} large-cap stock funds — the flagship and ESG-labeled funds of Vanguard, BlackRock, and State Street, two other large managers (Fidelity and Capital Group), and six ESG-dedicated fund families — to ask where stewardship actually happens. The short answer: inside a big family, an ESG fund votes almost exactly like that family's flagship; the real divide is between managers; and support for environmental and social proposals fell at most managers between 2024 and 2026, though the data can't fully separate weaker investor support from a changing ballot.

What these numbers can and can't say

  • Small samples. Some rates rest on few votes. Charts show the number of votes and a 95% interval in their tooltips, and the text says so where a figure is thin.
  • Proposal direction is judged by keyword rules. Whether a shareholder proposal is “pro-ESG” or “anti-ESG” comes from ordered keyword rules, not from a person reading each one. Every label is listed for review.
  • Association, not motive. The filings show how funds voted, not why. Nothing here explains a fund's policy.
  • {{head.funds}} funds, U.S. large-cap. This is a deliberately chosen panel, not every fund or every manager.
Labels

Inside a fund family, the ESG fund votes the same way as the flagship

Each of the Big Three, and Fidelity, sells ESG-labeled versions of its U.S. index funds. On the same shareholder proposal at the same meeting, those ESG funds cast a different vote from their family's flagship almost never: {{sib.ishaware}}% of the time for iShares ESG Aware ({{sib.ishaware.n}} proposals compared), {{sib.spdresg}}% for SPDR S&P 500 ESG, {{sib.vanesg}}% for Vanguard ESG U.S. Stock, and {{sib.fidsust}}% for Fidelity's U.S. Sustainability Index. The two exceptions are small: iShares Paris-Aligned Climate differs on {{sib.ishparis}}% of shareholder proposals, and Fidelity's actively managed Sustainable U.S. Equity Fund on {{sib.fidsusteq}}% compared with Contrafund.

For scale, the Vanguard 500 Index Fund and the Calvert U.S. Large-Cap Core Responsible Index Fund — an ESG index fund from a different manager — disagree on {{ref}}% of the {{ref.n}} shareholder proposals they both voted (95% interval {{ref.lo}}–{{ref.hi}}%). The label changes which companies a fund holds; the family's stewardship team decides how it votes. That is what a single, centralized voting policy predicts, so the finding is about where the vote is decided, not about whether ESG funds “should” vote differently. Across families, filers word the same proposal differently, so cross-family comparisons like the Calvert one match fewer proposals than same-family ones do.

Managers

The real divide is between managers: from 0% to 80% support for pro-ESG proposals

Counting only shareholder proposals that ask companies for more climate, social, or human-rights action or disclosure (“pro-ESG”), Vanguard's three funds supported none of them: {{p.van500.k}} of {{p.van500.n}} for the Vanguard 500 Index Fund. BlackRock's S&P 500, ESG Aware, and ESG Screened ETFs supported about 2% (its Paris-Aligned Climate ETF {{p.ishparis}}%), State Street's SPDR funds 9–11%, and Fidelity's index funds under 3%. At the other end, Calvert's index fund supported {{p.calidx}}%, Domini {{p.dom}}%, and Green Century {{p.gc}}%.

Actively managed mainstream funds sit in between but closer to the index giants: Fidelity Contrafund at {{p.fidcontra}}%, The Growth Fund of America at {{p.gfa}}%. Fidelity's Sustainable U.S. Equity Fund, at {{p.fidsusteq}}%, is the ESG fund inside a mainstream family that votes most differently from its sibling.

Funds hold different companies, so a fund could look different just because it faced different proposals. As a check, restricting every fund to meetings at S&P 500 companies ({{head.spm}} meetings where at least three of the four S&P 500 flagships voted) moves no fund's rate by more than {{robust.max}} points (Calvert's index fund goes from {{p.calidx}}% to {{p.calidx.m}}%), so the ranking is not an artifact of who holds what. Two funds rest on few votes: Calvert Equity ({{p.caleq.n}} pro-ESG votes, interval {{p.caleq.lo}}–{{p.caleq.hi}}%) and Parnassus ({{p.parn.n}} votes, interval {{p.parn.lo}}–{{p.parn.hi}}%).

Big Three

Big Three support for pro-ESG proposals, already low, kept falling

Among the four S&P 500 index flagships, only State Street's gave pro-ESG proposals meaningful support, and it fell from {{p.spdrport.s0}}% in the 2023–24 proxy year to {{p.spdrport.s1}}% and then {{p.spdrport.s2}}%. iShares went from {{p.ishcore.s0}}% to {{p.ishcore.s1}}% to {{p.ishcore.s2}}%, Fidelity's 500 Index Fund from {{p.fid500.s0}}% to zero, and Vanguard stayed at zero all three years. Each fund-year rests on {{bt.nmin}} to {{bt.nmax}} votes.

The ballot

Fewer environmental and social proposals reached a vote, and more of them were anti-ESG

The four S&P 500 index flagships hold nearly the same companies, so the proposals they vote on track the ballot itself. Each voted on about {{bal.0.total}} shareholder proposals in the 2023–24 proxy year, {{bal.1.total}} in 2024–25, and {{bal.2.total}} in 2025–26. Pro-ESG proposals fell faster, from about {{bal.0.pro}} to {{bal.2.pro}} — a {{bal.drop}}% drop.

Proposals pushing the other way — asking companies to report the risks of their DEI programs or climate commitments, or to review “politicized” banking — fell much less (from about {{bal.0.anti}} to {{bal.2.anti}}), so their share of environmental and social proposals rose from {{bal.0.share}}% to {{bal.2.share}}%.

Part of this is likely supply rather than investor demand, and this panel can't separate the two: it sees only proposals that reached a vote. In February 2025 SEC staff issued Staff Legal Bulletin 14M, which rescinded earlier guidance and made it easier for companies to exclude proposals from the proxy, and in November 2025 the staff announced it would not respond to most exclusion requests for the 2025–26 season. The timing fits a smaller ballot; the data can't show cause.

The ballot

Almost no fund votes for anti-ESG proposals — including the ones that vote against ESG proposals

Anti-ESG proposals received {{anti.big3}}% support from the Big Three funds ({{anti.big3.n}} votes) and {{anti.other}}% from the Fidelity and Capital Group funds ({{anti.other.n}} votes); with samples that size, the true rate is very likely under about 1%. The ESG families gave them {{anti.esgf}}% ({{anti.esgf.n}} votes, interval {{anti.esgf.lo}}–{{anti.esgf.hi}}%). So the Big Three's low support for pro-ESG proposals is not a mirror image: they vote against shareholder proposals on environmental and social topics from both directions.

ESG families

ESG specialists supported fewer pro-ESG proposals in 2025–26, mostly on climate and human rights

Calvert's index fund supported {{sp.calidx.0}}% of pro-ESG proposals in 2023–24 and {{sp.calidx.2}}% in 2025–26. Domini went from {{sp.dom.0}}% to {{sp.dom.2}}% and Green Century from {{sp.gc.0}}% to {{sp.gc.2}}%. Parnassus moved the other way, to {{sp.parn.2}}%, but on only {{sp.parn.2.n}} votes (interval {{sp.parn.2.lo}}–{{sp.parn.2.hi}}%), and Calvert Equity's {{sp.caleq.2}}% rests on {{sp.caleq.2.n}} — too few to read. Hollow points on the chart mark fund-years with fewer than 30 votes.

Is that just a changing ballot? Anti-ESG proposals are counted separately, but the pro-ESG proposals themselves may also have changed. Holding the topic fixed, the ESG families' support fell in 2025–26 for climate proposals ({{ts.env.1}}% to {{ts.env.2}}%, {{ts.env.2.n}} votes) and for human-rights and workforce proposals ({{ts.hr.1}}% to {{ts.hr.2}}%, {{ts.hr.2.n}} votes), but not for other social proposals ({{ts.oth.1}}% to {{ts.oth.2}}%); the diversity figure ({{ts.dei.1}}% to {{ts.dei.2}}%) rests on {{ts.dei.2.n}} votes. Restricting to S&P 500 meetings gives the same overall path ({{ts.sp.0}}%, {{ts.sp.1}}%, {{ts.sp.2}}%). So the decline is not just a topic-mix effect, but the panel can't rule out that the proposals still reaching a vote differ in quality or wording from earlier years.

Impax's 2025–26 filing is not shown: its vote column appears to hold management's recommendations rather than the fund's votes (see “About the data”). In the two years it reported cleanly, Impax supported {{sp.impax.lo}}–{{sp.impax.hi}}% of pro-ESG proposals.

Pay & boards

Pushback on pay and boards comes from a few ESG families and Capital Group's Growth Fund, not the index giants

On say-on-pay votes, Domini voted against {{pay.dom}}% of the time, Calvert's index fund {{pay.calidx}}%, and Green Century {{pay.gc}}%. On director elections, Domini voted against or withheld on {{dir.dom}}% of nominees and Green Century {{dir.gc}}%. The ESG families are not uniform, though: Parnassus opposed {{pay.parn}}% of pay packages and {{dir.parn}}% of nominees, and Impax {{pay.impax}}% and {{dir.impax}}%.

Among mainstream funds, The Growth Fund of America opposed {{pay.gfa}}% of pay packages ({{pay.gfa.m}}% at S&P 500 meetings) but only {{dir.gfa}}% of director nominees; Fidelity's funds and Washington Mutual look much like the Big Three, where every fund opposed {{pay.big3max}}% or fewer pay packages and {{dir.big3max}}% or fewer director nominees. Restricting to S&P 500 meetings changes several of these figures by several points (Green Century's say-on-pay opposition goes from {{pay.gc}}% to {{pay.gc.m}}%), so treat the exact percentages as approximate; the tooltips show both.

Governance

The Big Three do back shareholder proposals — on governance, not environmental or social issues

Big Three funds supported {{gv.big3.rights}}% of shareholder proposals on shareholder rights and defenses (proxy access, special meetings, declassified boards), about {{gv.big3.ratio}} times their {{gv.big3.pro}}% support for pro-ESG proposals. Fidelity and Capital Group show the same pattern, and it helps to keep index and active funds apart: Fidelity's two index funds backed {{gv.idx.rights}}% of shareholder-rights proposals ({{gv.idx.rights.n}} votes) and {{gv.idx.pro}}% of pro-ESG ones; the four active funds backed {{gv.act.rights}}% ({{gv.act.rights.n}} votes) and {{gv.act.pro}}%. Those shareholder-rights figures are small samples. Only the ESG families support both kinds at similar rates ({{gv.esgf.rights}}% and {{gv.esgf.pro}}%).

About the data

Source. SEC Form N-PX filings, pulled from EDGAR by scripts/fetch_npx.js. Since July 2023 funds file N-PX in a structured format that records, for every proposal, the company, meeting date, proposal text, the SEC's topic categories, who proposed it, how the fund voted, and whether the vote was for or against management. Each filing covers one July 1–June 30 reporting year; this panel covers the three years ending June 2024, 2025, and 2026.

What one row is. One fund's vote on one proposal at one company meeting: {{head.votes}} rows for {{head.funds}} funds, {{head.companies}} companies (share classes counted once), and {{head.quarters}} calendar quarters of meetings ({{head.firstQuarter}} to {{head.lastQuarter}}). The funds are all U.S. large-cap stock funds, chosen so they vote at largely the same companies.

Fund groups. “Big Three” is Vanguard, BlackRock (iShares), and State Street (SPDR). “Other large managers” is Fidelity and Capital Group; it mixes index funds (Fidelity 500 Index, Fidelity U.S. Sustainability Index) with active ones, so where the difference matters the report splits it by style. “ESG families” is Parnassus, Calvert, Domini, Impax, and Green Century.

Cleaning. Some filers report one proposal in several blocks (one per voting team); those are merged into one row, and a block that exactly repeats another is counted once. Shares some investors vote under their own chosen policy (pass-through voting) appear as small separate blocks; any block under 10% of the fund's largest block at a meeting is left out, so the data reflects the fund manager's own decision. Vote spellings are normalized. Details and counts are in the repository README.

Rows dropped, and why. Despite its name, the N-PX field labeled “management recommendation” records whether each vote was for or against management; with-management rates use it that way. One filing is left out: Impax's 2025–26 report (1,855 rows) shows FOR on every management item and AGAINST on 142 of 143 shareholder proposals — management's usual position everywhere — while its own for/against-management field marks 114 director votes as against management. The two fields contradict each other, so the filing is excluded rather than reinterpreted; its rows remain in data/votes.csv.gz.

Proposal direction. The SEC categories say what a proposal is about but not which way it points. scripts/stance_rules.js labels each shareholder proposal pro-ESG, anti-ESG, unclear (topics both camps file, such as charitable giving), or governance, using ordered keyword rules; every label records the rule that produced it, and data/proposal_stance_review.csv lists all {{cls.texts}} distinct proposal texts for review. {{audit.sentence}} Proposals no rule recognized ({{cls.norule}} of {{cls.gov}} votes in the governance-and-other group, {{cls.norule.pct}}%) are counted as governance; the Capital Group finding is shown both ways.

How the rates are computed. Support is the share of votes cast FOR, out of FOR, AGAINST, ABSTAIN, and WITHHOLD. Opposition on management items is the share of those votes that were not FOR. Rates pool fund-level votes, so a proposal voted by three funds counts three times. Intervals are 95% Wilson score intervals on the pooled votes; they treat votes as independent, which they are not (funds in one family vote alike), so read them as a floor on the uncertainty. Head-to-head comparisons match proposals by company, meeting date, and proposal text.

Same ballot check. A “S&P 500 meeting” is a company meeting at which at least three of the four S&P 500 flagships voted. Restricting every fund to these meetings compares funds on nearly the same proposals.

Reproducibility. Every figure and every number in the written findings is computed in scripts/analyze.js into data/findings.json, and the page text is filled in from that output by scripts/build_report.js, which also checks the report's verbal claims (for example, “supported none of them”) against the data before it will build.
